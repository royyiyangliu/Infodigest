Sebastian Cardoso: What Happens to Game Studios After an Acquisition
Sebastian Cardoso worked at Riot, Crytek, and EA, and
writes the Game Studio Unlocked newsletter
. He collected data on 392 PC and console studios that large companies have acquired since 2000 and assessed each one's fate using public sources. The data is current as of October 30, 2026. The author continues to update the dataset after publication, so some figures may have shifted slightly.
Each studio gets two grades. For its games:
Thrived
(better reviews or sales than before the deal), Stable (about the same),
Declined
(clearly worse reviews, sales, or both), and
Shuttered
(the studio was closed). For the organization (operating studios only):
Intact
(no mass layoffs, absorption, or leadership exodus),
Strained
(one serious hit: a layoff, the founders leaving, or a canceled flagship product), and
Damaged
(a layoff of 15%+ of staff, three or more layoff rounds in seven years, absorption into the parent company, or reassignment to support work on other teams’ games).

Games and Organization After the Deal
Of the 362 acquired developers, 95 (26.2%) saw their games get worse after the deal, and another 123 (34%) were shut down. That means things got worse for 60.2% of studios after an acquisition. 45 studios (12.4%) did better, and nothing changed for 99 studios (27.3%).
❗️It’s worth noting that 39 of the 95 studios ended up in the
Declined
category not because they released bad games. They were absorbed by the parent company, lost their own identity, or were moved to support work on other teams’ projects. These include major developers like Bungie, Rare, Obsidian, and id Software. If such cases aren’t counted as a decline, the share of
Declined
and
Shuttered
studios drops to 49.4%.
After an acquisition, sales fall more often than review scores. Sales declined for 57.3% of studios (and rose for 25.7%); critic review scores fell for 31.3% (and rose for 19.9%).
Cardoso explains that game quality depends on the team, which stays in place for a while after the deal. Marketing, pricing, platforms, and release timing, meanwhile, are controlled by the owner. For example, id Software’s Doom: The Dark Ages reviewed well in 2025 but reportedly sold far worse than Doom Eternal. The game launched straight into Game Pass.
Of the 239 studios still operating, 109 (45.6%) continue unchanged, 61 (25.5%) took one serious hit, and 69 (28.9%) underwent major structural changes. Cardoso notes that the last number is likely understated: large companies often report the total number of people laid off, but not which studios they came from.
In the end, only 103 of the 362 studios (28.5%) can be called healthy: their games either improved or stayed the same, and the company didn’t undergo a reorganization. In other words, things work out well after a deal for roughly one studio in four.
The earlier a studio was acquired, the worse the outcome. Among studios acquired before 2010, 84.6% saw declining results or closure. Among those acquired in 2018-2021, 42.5%.
30 studios in the sample did better after the acquisition and had no layoffs. They include FromSoftware (Kadokawa), Atlus (Sega), and Warhorse (Embracer). According to Cardoso, they have one thing in common: the buyer acquired a successful studio and left it alone. Since 2014, under Kadokawa, FromSoftware has released Bloodborne, Sekiro, and Elden Ring (30 million copies) without a single reported layoff.
62 studios in the sample started releasing worse games and went through serious structural changes. In the author’s view, this is the most typical scenario of a failed acquisition. This group includes Crystal Dynamics, Eidos-Montreal, and Firaxis.
For another seven studios, Cardoso uses the term
Hollowed
: the games are successful, but the studio lays people off. For example, Apex Legends has been one of the biggest live games for years, while Respawn went through three rounds of cuts over the same period. All layoff news at these studios dates from 2023 onward.
Creative Assembly has a similar story. In the financial year ending March 2025, the studio’s average headcount fell from 683 to 383 people, and its wage bill from £53.1 million to £27.2 million. The Total War series, meanwhile, continues to sell well.
Closures
Of the 362 studios, 123 were shut down. On average, 6.4 years pass between acquisition and closure; the median is 5 years. 70 of the 123 studios were closed within five years of the deal.
Many well-known studios were closed quickly. EA closed Pandemic two years after acquiring it, Sony closed Firewalk roughly a year and a half in, and Warner Bros. closed Player First roughly seven months in.
Counting from the date of acquisition, 19.7% of the 315 studios acquired through 2021 were closed within five years of the deal. Of the 180 studios acquired by 2016, 39.4% were closed within ten years.
From 2019 to 2022, only two acquired studios were closed. From 2023 through October 2026, 41 studios were closed. Adding the studios companies founded themselves and the VR studios they acquired, 71 studios closed in 46 months.
Cardoso links this to the fact that most of the 2020-2022 acquisitions were made against a backdrop of cheap, easy money and the pandemic bubble. When interest rates rose and engagement returned to its previous level, many studios became a burden for their owners.
The biggest name in the dataset is BioWare. EA closed the studio on October 21, 2026, 19 years after acquiring it. Before that, layoffs had shrunk it from 400+ people to fewer than 100.
Who Buys Studios
According to Cardoso, the main factor that determines a studio’s fate is who bought it. Here he counts ownership periods rather than studios: if a studio changed owners, it is counted once for each owner. For example, Bungie’s years with Microsoft (2000-2007) and with Sony (since 2022) are counted separately. There are 414 such periods in total.
At Activision Blizzard, all 19 ownership periods ended with either the studio’s closure or a decline in its performance. The acquired teams (Raven, Neversoft, Vicarious Visions, High Moon, and Beenox) were moved to support Call of Duty. After the Guitar Hero collapse, six more studios were closed.
Disney (5 companies), Midway (4 companies), and Bandai Namco (3 companies) also have a 100% negative record. Next come THQ (90% of the studios under its ownership went through hard times), EA, and Thunderful (88% each).
Large publishers and platform holders have mixed results: at Take-Two, 70% of acquired studios started performing worse or were closed; at Ubisoft and Warner Bros., 67% each; at Microsoft, 63%; and at Sony, 58%. At Embracer, the owner of the largest portfolio in the dataset (44 companies), it’s 43%.
Nintendo (6 companies), Sumo Group (6 companies), and Kadokawa (3 companies) have no failures. Over 24 years, Nintendo acquired six studios and didn’t close a single one.
❗️Cardoso himself notes that the sample has its quirks. In some cases, two years of data are set against twenty, and comparing them isn’t entirely fair. Of the 25 ownership cases under four years old, not a single one has ended in decline or closure yet. For cases 4-7 years old, the share of such outcomes is about 40%; for 13 years or more, about 75%.
Cardoso explains the difference this way. The worst results belong to companies that bought studios to feed their own pipeline (Activision for Call of Duty, THQ for licensed games) or bought them with debt (Embracer, Nacon). Nacon, for example, entered insolvency proceedings in February 2026 and has since lost Spiders, Big Bad Wolf, and Midgar. The best results belong to those who buy studios they have worked with for a long time and leave the existing team in charge.
20 studios bought themselves back from their owner or were spun out as independent companies. None of them have declined or closed since. They include IO Interactive (which left Square Enix), Coffee Stain (from Embracer), and Toys for Bob (from Activision). Cardoso notes, though, that only a studio that is already doing well can afford to buy itself out.
Which Studios Get Closed More Often
The bigger the studio, the less often it gets closed. Of the 47 flagship studios (150+ people), 8.5% were closed; of the 168 mid-sized studios (40-150 people), 35.7%; and of the 147 small studios (under 40 people), 40.1%.
That said, flagship companies aren’t necessarily doing well. 36.2% of flagships saw declining results or closure (vs. 65.5% of mid-sized and 61.9% of small studios). Instead of closing them, owners more often cut or absorb flagship studios, while the big franchise the company is known for keeps shipping and making money. For example, Blizzard hasn’t released a single new franchise since 2016.
At the time of the deal, 30 of the 392 acquired studios mainly did co-development, ports, and support work (for example, Nixxes, which makes PC ports for Sony). Cardoso counts them separately. Only one of them has been closed. However, none of them made it into the growing category either: a team booked solid with external contracts has little chance of releasing a hit of its own.

Studios that large companies founded themselves have even worse results than acquired ones. Of the 56 such studios, 85.7% had poor results, and 58.9% were closed. 18 of the 33 closed studios never released a single game.
NetEase founded eight studios in 2020-2023 (including Jar of Sparks, Worlds Untold, and Nagoshi Studio), and all eight are already closed. Microsoft closed The Initiative, which didn’t release anything in seven years.
Comparison With Independent Studios
For comparison, Cardoso assembled 50 notable independent PC and console studios (including Larian, CD Projekt Red, and Remedy). They are compared with 119 acquired studios that were already owned and operating as of October 2019. The period studied is seven years, from October 2019 to October 2026.
Over that time, 48% of independent studios had at least one round of layoffs, all of them in 2023-2026. Heavy layoffs (one cut of 15%+ of staff, or two of 10%+) hit 14% of independent studios (7 of 50) and 24.4% of acquired ones (29 of 119). Counting as heavy all the layoffs whose scale couldn’t be verified, the figures are 36% and 52.9%, respectively.
Over the seven years, one independent studio out of 50 (2%) closed: Intrepid, seven weeks after its early access launch. Among acquired studios, 18 of 119 (15.1%) were closed. That means acquired studios closed roughly 7.5 times more often.
❗️Cardoso himself notes that independent studios are less likely to report layoffs and closures, and only notable companies made it into the sample. So the real difference is most likely smaller.

All growth is still coming from non-gaming apps. They earned a record $25.6 billion (+15% YoY). Games, meanwhile, earned $19.4 billion (-6.9% YoY).
Compared with Q2'26, games grew slightly (+1% QoQ, from $19.2 billion). Sensor Tower calls this the first sign of stabilization after several consecutive quarters of decline.
On iOS, game revenue fell 9% YoY to $12 billion. On Google Play, games earned $7.38 billion (-3.4% YoY).

Game downloads are falling on both platforms: 9.64 billion on Google Play (-8.1% YoY) and 1.74 billion on iOS (-7.4% YoY).
Key Markets
❗️Country figures cover all apps, not just games. In China, Sensor Tower counts iOS only.
The US is the world's largest app market. In Q3'26, it reached $14.63 billion (-3% YoY). That's almost a third of global revenue. Compared with Q2'26, the market grew 2.4%.
China is second ($6.02 billion, +8% YoY), and Japan is third ($4.25 billion, +1% YoY). South Korea earned more than $2 billion for the first time ($2.09 billion, +12% YoY).
China (+$455 million), South Korea (+$222 million), and the UK (+$191 million, +11% YoY) grew the most in absolute terms. In Korea, the UK, and Germany, much of the growth came from utilities and generative AI.
India (6.73 billion, +3% YoY), the US (3.16 billion, -1.5% YoY), and Brazil (2.3 billion, -1.2% YoY) led in terms of downloads.
In absolute terms, India (+226 million), Indonesia (+127 million), and the Philippines (+117 million) shown the biggest download growth in Q3'26. African countries are also growing fast: Nigeria (+13% YoY) and Kenya (+24% YoY).
Key Categories: Game Genres and Apps
Strategy is still the biggest genre by revenue at $4.15 billion. But its revenue fell 20% when comparing Q3'26 with Q3'25. Sensor Tower attributes this to spending normalizing in Whiteout Survival and Last War: Survival after their 2025 peaks.
Puzzle grew 13% YoY to $4.14 billion. Only $10 million now separates it from Strategy. Merge games drove the growth: Gossip Harbor (+69% YoY) and Tasty Travels (+300% YoY).
By subgenre, Match Swap ($1.73 billion) is still well ahead of Match Merge 2 ($1.13 billion).
Among the ten largest genres by revenue, only Sports (+20% YoY), Tabletop (+7% YoY), and Simulation (+1% YoY) grew, besides Puzzle. All other genres are declining. Excluding Strategy, RPG (-15% YoY) and Casino (-14% YoY) saw the steepest revenue drops.
❗️Some spending in these genres has most likely moved to D2C. Sensor Tower doesn't count that revenue in its report.
By downloads, only Puzzle (2.59 billion, +8% YoY) and Sports (+11% YoY) are growing among the top 10 genres. Racing (-22% YoY) and Shooter (-19% YoY) fell the most.
Puzzle's download growth in Q3'26 came largely from arrow puzzles (Easybrain's Arrow Puzzle and Arrows GO!) and Meowdoku.
The 2026 FIFA World Cup helped Sports games. All five of the genre's most-downloaded games are soccer games. Sports game downloads grew the most in Mexico (+41% YoY), Argentina (+33% YoY), and Brazil (+21% YoY).
Generative AI apps grew 103% YoY and entered the top three app categories by revenue, behind social media and video streaming. ChatGPT became the world's top-grossing app for the first time, overtaking TikTok. Claude is 8th.
Video streaming apps are growing the fastest in downloads (+20% YoY), largely thanks to vertical micro-drama apps, which took half of the top 10 spots by download growth.

76% of respondents are younger than 45. 62% are men, and 28% are women (24% in the previous edition).
66% work at game studios. The share of AAA studio employees rose to 36% (from 31%), while the share of AA studio employees fell to 14% (from 18%). AAA is now represented in the survey on par with indies: 19% work at indie studios older than five years, and 16% at younger ones.
Game designers (a group that also includes narrative professionals), programmers, and artists make up the largest groups of respondents.
Layoffs
34% of respondents were laid off over the past two years. In the US, it's 39%; outside the US, 26%. In the previous edition, it was 28% across all respondents and 33% in the US.
The share of laid-off workers grew both in the US and outside it. So it's not just that there are more Americans in the sample.
❗️For comparison, according to estimates by ASGC (Amir Satvat's project), 14% of US game industry workers were laid off over the past 24 months. GDC itself notes that the survey disproportionately includes people affected by layoffs. Perhaps these people have more to share, and they take such surveys more often.
In the US, only 50% of respondents work full-time (59% last year). 21% are unemployed, vs. 13% a year ago.
68% of laid-off workers lost their jobs in the past 12 months. 79% were laid off once, 17% twice, and 3% three or more times.
QA, marketing, and PR staff, as well as artists, were laid off most often. Employees in business operations and at service companies were laid off least often.
49% say their company had layoffs over the past year. At AAA studios, it's 68%; at indie studios older than five years, 35%; and at younger ones, 14%. The main reasons are restructuring, financial difficulties, and canceled projects.
54% of laid-off workers still haven't found a new job (59% in the US). In the previous edition, it was 48%.
27% have sent at least 100 job applications. 41% used generative AI while looking for work; among those still searching, it's 47%.
Of those who found a job, 23% consider their new one better than the previous one, and 17% worse. 9% left the game industry, and 4% founded their own company or studio.
55% of employed respondents aren't confident they'll keep their current job over the next 12 months. Employees in business operations (63%) and at indie studios older than five years (54%) feel the most secure. Employees at younger indie studios are the least confident about their future (36%).
72% of unemployed respondents don't believe they'll find a job in the industry within a year.
87% of students are worried about their future careers (74% in the previous edition). Half of them say they don't have proper access to internships. 92% of educators note that the state of the industry is affecting graduates' job placement.
77% of US respondents aren't hopeful about where the industry is headed; outside the US, it's 66%. 30% of Americans and 23% of respondents from other countries are considering leaving the game industry within the next year.
Generative AI
40% of respondents use generative AI at work (36% a year ago). In the US, the figure barely changed (38% vs. 36%), while outside the US, it grew from 37% to 44%.
Men use AI more often (45%) than women (33%). Among people over 45, the share is 54%. By discipline, people in business operations (63%), marketing and PR (51%), and programming (48%) lead.
At studios working on F2P projects, 58% of employees use AI; at mobile studios, 70%. At studios developing premium games, it's 32%.
27% of AAA studio employees say they're encouraged or pressured to use AI, and another 3% are required to use it. At indie studios, it's 9%.
57% of AI users work with it daily. The most popular tools are text generation (72%), code generation (62%), and AI in productivity software (35%). 22% use image generation, and 7% use video generation.
73% use AI for research and brainstorming, 62% for data analysis and summarization, and 56% for writing code and debugging. 22% use AI for concept art, and 8% for final in-game assets.
55% believe generative AI has a negative impact on the game industry. In the January edition, it was 54% (GDC corrected last edition's figure; the report originally said 52%). 8% consider AI's impact positive, and another 35% mixed.
QA and community support staff have the most negative view of AI (76% negative), followed by game designers and artists (65% each).
GDC notes that attitudes toward AI have become less black-and-white. Many see value in automating routine tasks, research, and prototyping, but oppose AI use being visible to players. This year, responses also included concerns that AI leads to skill atrophy.
Engines and Platforms
Questions about engines and platforms were asked only to "developers": game designers, programmers, and artists (about 1,200 people).
35% of developers use Unreal Engine (42% a year ago), and 28% use Unity (down from 30%). The share of proprietary engines rose from 19% to 23%. Godot is at 6%.
GDC checked whether this is related to the larger share of AAA in the sample. Unreal Engine and Unity usage declined across all studio types, while proprietary engines became more popular at AAA, AA, and indie studios older than five years. 11% of indie studio employees use Godot (up from 7%).
81% of developers plan to release their next game on PC. Next come PS5 (45%), Xbox Series X/S (37%), Steam Deck (32%), and Nintendo Switch 2 (29%).
21% are making a game for the Steam Machine. 10% plan to launch on the next PlayStation, and 8% on the next Xbox (Project Helix).
The platforms developers are most interested in are PC (81%), Nintendo Switch 2 (46%), and PS5 (42%). In the previous edition, Steam Deck (40%) and Switch 2 (39%) were nearly tied; now Switch 2 has pulled ahead (Steam Deck is at 38%).
29% of developers are interested in the Steam Machine, 27% in the next PlayStation, and 17% in Project Helix. Interest in developing for Xbox Series X/S (26%) is on par with the next PlayStation.
VR keeps losing developers. 3% are working on VR/AR/MR projects (8% in the previous edition), and interest in the platform fell from 12% to 8%.
Business
75% of studio employees make premium games, and 25% make F2P games.
❗️GDC itself points out that the sample is skewed toward North America. According to Sensor Tower, of the 225,000 games released in 2025, 91% were mobile, and 96% of those were F2P.
In F2P games, the main revenue sources are in-game currency (65%), cosmetics (60%), and battle passes (48%). 27% use gacha and loot boxes. In premium, the core revenue sources are digital sales (91%) and physical copies (40%); only 41% of paid games have an additional revenue stream in the form of DLC.
The most common launch price is $10-19.99 (22% of games). Next come $20-29.99 (16%) and $60-69.99 (14%).
70% of AAA studios price their games at $50 or more. 60% of AA projects cost $30-60, and 70% of games from indie studios older than five years cost $10-40. 61% of younger indie studios sell their games for under $20.
50% develop projects with the studio's own money, including 35% of AAA studio employees. Next on the list of funding sources are publishing deals (15%), co-development (9%), and private investment (5%). Only 4% make games with venture capital. In the previous edition, 35% of respondents were self-funded, and 20% had publishing deals.
When choosing a platform, studios look at audience reach (77%), discoverability (48%), and business model success on the platform (47%).
45% of studio employees say their company hired co-development contractors for its most recent project. Among AAA studio employees, 64% said so; at younger indie studios, 23%. Art, animation, and engineering are outsourced most often.
7% of respondents work at co-development studios, and almost half of them (48%) also make their own games. In addition, 16% of employees at other studios say their company provided co-development services over the past year. Among respondents at indie studios older than five years, 23% said so.

Working Conditions
❗️GDC returned to the previous answer options for working hours, so there's no comparison with last year here.
56% of developers work 40 hours a week or less. For other industry workers (marketing, business, etc.), it's the opposite: 65% work more than 40 hours.
86% of developers worked more than 40 hours in a single week at least once over the past year. 23% of developers reached 60+ hours, and 13% reached 70+. Notably, employees in other roles face extreme overtime (56+ hours) more often.
The main reasons for overtime are personal desire or work necessity (46%). 16% say they were pushed into it, and 11% have several jobs or contracts.
65% say their job has affected their mental health over the past year, and 32% strongly agree. Among the unemployed, it's 81%.
At the same time, 66% say they maintain a healthy work-life balance, and 70% manage their workload within working hours.
86% get more fulfillment from working in games than they would in another industry.
80% of US respondents support unionization (82% a year ago). But only 55% now want to join a union, down from 62%. 20% aren't interested in unions (up from 16%).
10% are members of an industry-wide union like United Videogame Workers-CWA, and 5% are members of a company union (2% a year ago).
28% of developers work on accessibility features (33% in the previous edition). The most common are button remapping (64%), color options (57%), and difficulty levels (51%).

Newzoo: Game Localization on PC and Console in 2026
Newzoo relies on data for 5,581 major games across 38 countries and 22 languages. To count languages, it also used data on 65,965 Steam games, with the language list taken from each game’s store page.
How Many Languages Games Support
In 2025, the PC and console markets were nearly equal in size: $43.6 billion and $44.7 billion, respectively. Meanwhile, 943.5 million people play on PC, and 632.4 million play on console.
Newzoo notes that the two platforms’ audiences only partly overlap. They often live in different countries and have different preferences. So a language that isn’t popular on one platform can be important for commercial success on the other.

Over two years, the PC audience grew by 66.4 million people, mostly thanks to China, India, and Brazil. Of these countries’ languages, only Chinese is part of the standard localization set for most games.
Almost every game ships in English. The typical Steam game supports just two languages, because most releases on the platform are small projects. The top 1,000 Steam games (which account for 69% of players), however, have a median of 10 languages. Newzoo considers this the benchmark for any mid-size or large project.
From 2015 to 2025, games with more than 15,000 lifetime PC players doubled the average number of languages they support. Among major games, the number of supported languages grew by roughly 74%.
Newzoo attributes this to cheaper translation, thanks to localization platforms and machine translation. Analysts expect generative AI to push the number of languages even higher. But poor localization in an official release undermines player trust.
The language set itself changed the most. In 2017, Chinese overtook German, French, and Spanish, and in 2021, Japanese followed. Of the 2025 Steam releases with 15,000+ players, 82.7% support Chinese.
Chinese text is available in 59.1% of major games, while China accounts for 28.2% of all PC players. Newzoo believes that even with this coverage, the Chinese market is still underserved. Formally, China’s PC market is closed to foreign publishers, but thanks to global Steam, the country’s importance to developers and publishers worldwide is growing.

Where Localization Pays Off
The multiplier shows how many times a country’s share of a game’s audience grows if the game ships in that country’s language. A 2x multiplier, for example, means that the share of a certain group of players (say, Japanese players) in the project’s total audience doubled.
On average, translating the text increases the share of players who speak that language by 2.11x on console and 1.41x on PC (across 22 languages).
❗️I couldn’t find specifics in the report on how these figures were calculated. The main problem with localization effectiveness is that it’s very hard to evaluate its impact retrospectively.
Newzoo explains the difference by the fact that console players play more premium single-player games, where language matters more.
For example, adding Korean localization on console can increase the share of Korean users by 3.10x. In other words, in games with Korean text, the share of players from Korea is more than three times higher than in games without it. On PC, the figure for Korean is 1.42x.
On PC, localization has a greater impact where English proficiency is lower. According to the methodology, Thai and Indonesian have the biggest effect. Translating into Dutch, however, does almost nothing, since the Netherlands has the highest English proficiency in the sample.
❗️That said, these markets are small in absolute terms, so localization is unlikely to pay off.
Japan is the exception. On PC, Japanese has a low multiplier because the Japanese Steam audience is small and fairly comfortable with English, according to the research. On console, where most Japanese players are, adding Japanese delivers the highest growth in the local players’ share compared with other languages.
French, German, and Spanish are in roughly 70% of games on PC and 85% on console, with Italian slightly behind. These four languages are the standard of modern localization.
Indonesian, however, is in only 2.7% of major PC games; Thai is in 7.7%, Arabic in 8.4%, Czech in 12.3%, and Turkish in 19.6%. For comparison, German is at 71.7%. Yet, according to Newzoo’s methodology, adding any of these languages increases the share of local players more than adding a major European language.
Newzoo notes that Arabic is one of the hardest languages to localize, both technically and professionally. Publishers who can handle this challenge will stand out from competitors.
Localization impact depends on the genre. For Japanese on console, the multiplier ranges from 4.94x in adventure games to 8.98x in RPGs. For Korean in RPGs, it’s 6.69x.
Newzoo explains this by saying story-driven and single-player games benefit more from translation: a player with weak English can figure out a shooter but may not follow a complex story. Newzoo, however, goes on to disprove its own hypothesis: when Japanese is added to shooters, the multiplier (4.98x) is slightly higher than in adventure games.
Voiceover is the most expensive part of localization, and publishers often voice games in a single language (almost always English). Large AAA projects often have voiceover in several languages.
On PC, voiceover has almost no effect: text translation is enough for most players. On console, however, there is a return, especially for Japanese: local voiceover increases the share of the Japanese audience by 2.11x on console vs. 1.04x on PC.
Languages are rarely added after release. When Disco Elysium added Turkish text in late 2022, Turkey’s share of new players jumped from 3.2% in 2021 to 16.7% in 2022. Newzoo notes, though, that the game was already popular with Turkish players and cost $3.30 because it was priced in local currency, Turkish lira.
Voiceover added after release had no significant effect in the cases Newzoo studied.
