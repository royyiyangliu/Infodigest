Introduction
For the past three months, the Citrini Research team has been working with Anthropic’s internal research model
1
to see if we could create novel proteins in a computer that would do what we wanted them to in an actual lab.
On the morning of August 12th, I woke to a Slack notification that our autonomous drug design campaign was concluding and the results were being compiled.
The previous day, from a nondescript conference room in the Anthropic offices, we prompted Claude to go from an idea to a package ready to submit to a physical lab. Overnight, Claude spawned more than 300 sub-agents using more than 300 concurrent GPUs to design, score, and deliver a set of proteins. At our direction, it sent them to the lab for synthesis and testing without any further input from us.
“But I thought you guys did, like, stocks and stuff?”,
you may be questioning.
In early June,
we began searching for opportunities outside of AI infrastructure, concerned about crowding. One particular area struck us as asymmetric:
Life Sciences & Healthcare.
The sector had endured the reversal of the pandemic boom and a difficult funding environment as investors found more exciting places for their money. Over the past half-decade, it has been a graveyard for returns.
While everyone focused on which Japanese semi-doodad-producer would sell out next
2

secular story at cyclical lows.
Finally, our chance to write an article offering a reprieve from the threats of a dystopian-AI-future dominating the news (not that
we

none of us could claim to know anything about AI as it related to biology.

Proposals abound! Rare childhood diseases, a treatment for C. diff (a diarrhea-causing bacterium that plagues skilled nursing facilities with a smell that is burned into my memory from my time as an EMT), a “vaccine” that blocked powerful fentanyl analogs. But choosing a disease and choosing a testable design problem were two different things.
We wanted to give the model a challenge where molecular design mattered and something truly novel was being attempted, but also one where the underlying biology was sufficiently established and a lab could tell us whether we had made progress without first building an entirely new experimental system.

Through two passes, antivenoms kept surviving all those criteria.
The Winner: Scorpion Antivenom
The first candidate, snakebite envenomation, had
been worked on using AI by the Baker lab
. This gave us a scientific precedent and the prospect of feedback from someone who had done this professionally. We wanted to attempt something new, though, so we turned our focus from snake to scorpion toxins.
Antivenom also offered an acute treatment setting. A candidate intended for one or a few doses faces different development hurdles than a protein administered chronically
14
.We could plausibly create something that was a lot closer to the path for an actual product than with other conditions.
As a final push
over the edge for Scorpion Antivenom
,
Analyst #3 proposed that once we’d gotten the lab data back, he was willing to volunteer to cut to the chase, skip the animal trials and get stung by a scorpion to see if it worked - provided we conducted this exercise outside of a hospital that stocked the FDA-approved antivenom.
15
Existing scorpion antivenoms are the result of a process that involves injecting horses with controlled doses of venom that are harvested from live scorpions, collecting their plasma and then purifying the antibodies they produce.
At
$39 million per gallon
, scorpion venom is considered the
most valuable liquid in the world.
It is a laborious process from scorpion to stable to hospital, and US prices are in the thousands of dollars per dose (although much cheaper in Mexico). At scale, a cocktail of synthetically produced proteins would be cheaper. And the horses, for their part, deserve some reprieve.
Ultimately, we decided on a minibinder addressing scorpion toxins. A minibinder is a small protein that binds a target protein, like a toxin. The toxins were identifiable proteins, and designing a binder against an individual toxin would be useful evidence of what AI could enable.
Why a protein minibinder?
One of our first lessons was on the kind of molecule we should make if we wanted the highest likelihood of actually succeeding. The state of AI for large molecules (like insulin or antibodies - large chains of amino acids folded into intricate shapes) is much more advanced than for small molecules (like aspirin, organic chemical compounds). Proteins are a structured design problem with an evolutionary alphabet.
We ensured that we’d be able to source the toxins from the wet-lab and set Claude to work doing research on these targets.
Our first objective was a binder that would work against a single toxin, followed by a test of whether it actually blocked toxin activity.
We added a
moonshot
to give Claude a chance at a breakthrough. Claude cautioned us that a single molecule designed to bind all 5 toxins was very likely to fail. Still, we figured it was worth a shot to see if AI could accomplish something that was currently viewed as impossible.
Luckily, Anthropic was willing to play ball on the compute/token bill and go for it as well.
Agents Working Through the Night
From there, we set to work creating the prompt that would produce the package to send to the lab. Or, rather, we spent the day using Claude to design our prompt, along with the Anthropic team ensuring we avoided traps they’d become familiar with.
16
Our instructions grew into a roughly 35-page prompt, essentially all of which was prepared with Claude. The prompt specified the targets, design constraints, evaluation tools, and allocation of candidates.
The agents used an extensive body of human science and specialist tools with a degree of persistence and coordination that our team obviously could not have supplied. Those specialist tools
17
performed much of the underlying molecular computation, but Claude made them usable as a connected (and extremely sped-up) research process.
We could describe the objective and drill down on the reasoning, but we didn’t need to personally know how to operate every piece of software required to get there.
While not everything from the moment we hit enter went smoothly, nothing was smoother than the 24-hour period between sending the initial prompt and being presented with the designs and a very nicely formatted “hero PDF” describing what Claude had done and accomplished (as well as what it hadn’t).
Claude searched the literature, coordinated specialist design and prediction software, managed computational resources and assembled the candidate package. If it ran into issues or needed additional information from vendors, it could email them.
And it did!
At one point, under our supervision, we had Claude draft and send an email to the scientist at Twist in charge of procurement. In the email, Claude asked a numbered set of manufacturing-feasibility questions about what fusion and bispecific construct formats Twist could actually build and express. The subset of fusion designs was paused until the email was answered hours later, after we had left for the day, and Claude integrated the response and continued the design according to what it had learned.
Around 4pm, we returned our visitor badges to security and went home. The Claude plugin in our Slack channel would alert us if anything went wrong.
We had asked Claude to compile a report
18
that would show simulated predictions of whether these molecules would actually stick the way we wanted them to.
Seeing the first protein designs on the screen was, honestly, surreal. We were already much further than I figured we’d be in a campaign that genuinely was entirely AI-run up to this point.
The initial campaign resulted in 180 designs and ended 64 hours after it began, including the four follow-up prompts that grew the pool of designs to 211 we ultimately sent for synthesis in a lab, and our best design (Cn2-B1) was produced during the third follow-up. In total, t
he entirety of the design process that produced the original 180 designs involved a total of five messages sent by humans to Claude.
Overall, we sent 367 messages to Claude
19
. The entire design campaign used a little more than 10,000 H100 GPU-hours.
By the end of Claude’s run, we had structures, rankings, vendor specifications, and a full report that looked like it belonged in an academic journal.
We could (and did) message Claude asking to inspect them in three dimensions or to explain its reasoning.
Our picture of an AI assistant for biology research was becoming more akin to a coordinated dry-lab team inside a computer. There was a significant temptation to treat that as a result, but luckily for our scientific integrity we had already arranged to pay people to see if we should disagree with the computer. None of what we’d done so far was enough to qualify whether Claude had actually accomplished anything of note.
We’d need the real world for that.
The Digital-to-Physical Transition
We hit enter on our 35-page prompt on one of those August afternoons in New York that’s so oppressively humid it makes you wonder how anything got done before the invention of air conditioning.
By the time we had enough data to publish anything remotely scientifically respectable, the highs were in the 60s and the first Halloween stores had reemerged from wherever they go for the rest of the year.
Our virtual biologist may have compressed a year’s worth of work into a couple days, but biology works at the speed of life. DNA had to be synthesized, proteins produced and purified, toxins obtained in usable form, and appropriate controls established. Our digital invention was now set to create work for human scientists.
In order to maximize the robustness of our data that we could feed back to Claude, we used the services of two CROs - Adaptyv Bio (private) and
Twist Bioscience (TWST US)

The CRO bill across the two for DNA synthesis, expression and purification,  binding measurements and a developability assay had come just shy of $150,000. We had spent $49,997 on compute (Claude managed to undershoot by $3), we’d need to spend more seeing if that compute actually generated something useful.

What the Results Showed (So Far)
Anthropic has published a much more technically in-depth
blog post

23

Anthropic's Technical Blog Post
For casuals like us, however…
There were three questions we needed the experiments to answer in order to feel good about what Claude did:
Did a designed protein attach to its target?
Did it appear to occupy a relevant part of that target?
Did it prevent the toxin from disrupting a cell’s function?
17 of our designs bound to the target
across
four out of five toxins.
These proteins had been designed
without
using laboratory feedback to improve them.
Thirty-five design-toxin pairs “hit”,
but only 17 passed our criteria.
24
We tested our binders in Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR), which is a fancy way of saying we attached the binders to a gold plate, washed toxins over them, and shined light on the surface. Affinity – how strongly the binders attach to the toxins, measured with a “Kd value” (the lower the better) — is only one part of the evidence that would suggest something promising.
The tests then compared these results to one or several
known antibodies
against specific types of toxins to see how our binder stacked up against them. These would act as positive controls and give us a sense of how our binders compared with existing antibodies.
Cn2 produced the strongest leads. Two designs, Cn2-B1 and Cn2-B2, bound tightly, with affinities in the low-nanomolar range. A known antibody also blocked the Cn2 binders in every competition test that could be scored, suggesting they attach to an overlapping or nearby part of the toxin. That is encouraging evidence about where they bind.
Like Claude had warned us, the moonshot failed – none of our designs qualified as binding more than one toxin. Of 91 designs aimed at all five, just one qualified, and only against AaH I.
What Would Come Next
No experiment has yet tested whether they actually stopped the toxin working. A good binding measurement does not establish that a binder actually blocks the toxin, remains in the body long enough, or can become a useful therapy.
The immediate next question would be whether our best binders can stop toxin activity. We would first establish that the toxin preparations disrupt the electrical signaling they are supposed to affect. Then a patch-clamp assay, which measures currents through cells’ ion channels, could test whether a binder prevents or reverses that disruption.
Those measurements, including the failures, would go back into Claude to guide another round of design and testing. For example, our strong result with Cn2-B1 could give us a starting point for a
regional Mexican scorpion antivenom
provided we got results that established it actually prevents toxin activity. We would assess other important toxins and add complementary binders wherever protection is missing.
25
Then, we’d move on to protection studies in suitable animal experiments. We would run studies to discover the effective dose, the time it takes to reach the target, how long the drug remains in the body, and whether it causes issues like unwanted immune reactions or toxicity. In parallel, we would need a stable formulation and a reliable way to manufacture consistent batches. The safety, activity and manufacturing evidence would support an application to begin human trials.
We began with an investment question and ended with newly designed proteins that were partly experimentally validated. A great deal of science made that possible and a great deal of science remains before anyone could call the result a treatment.
But
still…
The frontier has moved.
And we’re still an investment research firm, not a biotech. So, what was our takeaway as investors from our research into the space and escapade into drug discovery?
