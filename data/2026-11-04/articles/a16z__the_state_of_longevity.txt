The longevity movement is here to stay (no pun intended).
Not long ago, taking care of our health largely meant following our doctor’s advice and sticking to what our insurance covered. There has always been a fringe movement of biohackers and crunchy people taking a more hands-on approach, but that mindset has only recently moved into the mainstream.
Two things accelerated this shift. The first was the COVID-19 pandemic, which made us acutely aware of our mortality and the fragility of our health. It exposed the limits of our medical system and reminded us that a yearly physical doesn’t necessarily mean we’re healthy.
The second was ChatGPT. Suddenly everyone could ask questions about their health and make sense of complex medical information in simple English. Today, Americans are taking their health into their own hands, spending more time, money, and energy on health and longevity.
So what is coming next? To understand how people are taking charge of their health – and what they want – we surveyed 500 randomly selected consumers in the U.S. on their views about everything from peptides to toxins to brain-computer interfaces.
We’re also talking to people at the cutting edge of health and technology. Our upcoming podcast conversations will explore how they approach their own health and what the rest of us might learn from them. First up, Andrew Huberman walks us through a day in his life, from food and exercise to supplements and sleep.
For founders, this newfound consumer interest in health creates an opening. We think some of the biggest opportunities in longevity will go to companies that help people take action and manage their health over time.
Below, we combine our observations on the longevity market with our recent survey results to explore where the next great companies might be built.
Trends in Longevity
The race to become a “healthcare home” or the “Muse for healthcare”
Today, there is no single place to manage your healthcare. Your records are scattered across doctors’ offices, and getting care means navigating a mix of phone calls, patient portals, pharmacies, and labs.
Companies are racing to build the ultimate healthcare home – an app that knows your medical history and records, prescribes your annual labs and imaging, connects to your wearable, and uses AI to help you understand what’s going on. And when you need care, it connects you with virtual or in-person clinicians who can prescribe treatment or perform procedures as needed.
The leading contenders in this race have different head starts. Function Health has lab and imaging results that show how our health changes over time. Oura and WHOOP already have a place in our daily routines. General Medicine and Counsel start with the ability to deliver care. Ferry Health understands the fragmented landscape of where to get insurance-covered care. OpenAI, Anthropic, Amazon, and Apple are building on large audiences and products we already use.
The race is to become the first place a consumer turns to when they need
something
done
for their health. Asking AI a question about your health is useful, but arranging the next test, booking the appointment, and making sure the patient gets follow-up care and prescriptions is harder. As AI makes answers and interpretation cheaper, value will migrate to the companies that can figure out the action layer.
If this sounds like “Muse for healthcare,” that’s the idea. There is room to build very large companies around the healthcare needs that generalist AI assistants are unlikely to prioritize. The complexity of our healthcare system today makes this harder to build, but could also make it harder to replace.
We expect room for multiple winners. Almost everyone could use this kind of help.
From organic food to non-toxic everything
The MAHA movement has brought the toxins in our food, water, and everyday products to light, and consumer willingness to spend on “clean” non-toxic products has grown massively. Just a few years ago, the word “microplastics” was not in our lexicon; now our survey revealed that 63% of consumers are aware of the plastic particles building up in our bodies, 35% are concerned about them, and the biggest biohackers are
trying plasma exchange to remove microplastics from their blood
.
For consumers looking to reduce everyday exposures, the shopping list goes way beyond buying organic food into a broader focus on what we ingest
.
Our survey of Americans revealed lots of consumer change in this respect. 24% of respondents are swapping to nontoxic cookware, 39% are subbing out plastic for glass, and 70% are filtering their water.
That mindset extends beyond the kitchen and into the broader home. Healthmaxxers are buying air purifiers (37%), testing their homes for mold (18%), switching to organic-cotton clothing or bedding (17.5%), and even buying new natural or organic mattresses (11%). Even EMF emitted by wifi, cell phones, and microwaves is on consumers’ radar: 14% said they are trying to reduce it.
Consumer spending is moving faster than the evidence. People are willing to pay for peace of mind, even when the health benefit of a particular purchase is hard to measure. In our survey, 62% of respondents said they would pay more for products labeled as “non-toxic.” That creates opportunities across food, home goods, and personal care.
Peptides for all
GLP-1 receptor agonist medicines (e.g., semaglutide, tirzepatide, orforglipron, and others) have revolutionized health more than any drug in our lifetimes, transforming care for diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular health – and the promise of their benefits keeps growing.
The runaway success of this class of approved GLP-1s, growing comfort with at-home self-injection, and loosening regulation have also fueled a surge of interest in other
unregulated
peptides. Consumers are now experimenting with compounds
not
approved by the FDA that are marketed for recovery, performance, and appearance, including the pursuit of “looksmaxxing.” For example, BPC-157 is promoted for tissue repair; melanotan II for tanning.
But the market’s enthusiasm to date has outpaced the actual evidence. Many of these compounds are still missing strong human data that demonstrates safety and efficacy, and buying from unregulated suppliers
raises questions regarding what is actually in the vial.
And while HHS’s most recent policy eases enforcement against licensed pharmacies compounding six peptides under specified conditions, these peptides remain unapproved by the FDA, with unresolved questions about safety and efficacy. Despite the buzz, the single-digit billion compounded peptide market is dwarfed by even a single FDA-approved peptide: Eli Lilly’s tirzepatide, which generated nearly $44 billion in sales under the Mounjaro and Zepbound brands in the first nine months of 2026 alone.
We see the peptide trend unfolding along two distinct paths across biotech and healthcare. From the biotech side, we predict growth in the pipeline of rigorous, FDA-regulated peptide drugs – and growing enthusiasm from investors seeking to underwrite medicines that not only meet the traditional safety and efficacy bars, but could
also
have vast consumer appeal. This is an unnatural trend for the biotech sector: drugs are indicated based on clinical need, and historically the market has actually struggled to identify and treat patients efficiently. Peptide medicines with robust consumer pull can create an unusually efficient path to patients, and we suspect this dynamic could extend to other disease areas, including sleep, bone health, and cardiovascular health.
On the care delivery side, we could see a repeat of the GLP-1 cycle if additional peptide drugs demonstrate safety and efficacy and gain mainstream adoption. GLP-1s helped many care delivery companies grow, but most of the enterprise value accrued to pharma. Other peptides could follow a similar pattern in care delivery: first, dedicated prescribing companies may emerge, following the model of Calibrate, Found, and Weekend Health from the GLP-1 wave. Then, existing healthcare companies could add peptide prescriptions to attract new patients. But as more companies offer the same drugs, prescribing them could become less of a competitive advantage and more of a service patients expect. At that point, offering peptides may matter more for keeping existing patients from leaving than for attracting new ones.
Cholesterol could become dinner-table conversation
Cholesterol could become dinner-table conversation for a surprising reason: while heart disease is still America’s biggest silent killer, there’s rapidly growing evidence that we can understand risk, detect disease at early stages, and intervene with medications to not only prevent but actually
reverse
some of the plaque already in our arteries. This is giving consumers a compelling reason to take their heart health seriously before a cardiac event.
For LDL – the “bad” cholesterol – the evidence increasingly supports “lower is better,” especially for people at higher risk.
A study published this October
found that intensive treatment shrank plaque in the heart’s arteries by about a fifth over 18 months in people with heart disease. Getting cholesterol into the “normal” range may not be enough for your personal risk, and medication could provide additional protection.
New treatments could bring cholesterol management to a wider audience. PCSK9 inhibitors like Repatha help the liver clear LDL from the blood, but injections, cost, and insurance restrictions have limited their use. Only 21% of our survey respondents knew about these drugs. We expect that to change and for these drugs to enter the consumer zeitgeist as miracle drugs that lower cholesterol with few side effects: Lipfendra, the first PCSK9 inhibitor available as a daily pill, received FDA approval in July 2026.
Evidence also supports treating high-risk patients before their first heart attack or stroke. In people with diabetes and no known major artery disease, adding Repatha to existing treatment
lowered the estimated five-year risk of a first heart attack, stroke, or death from coronary heart disease from 7 to 5%
.
The next frontier is lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), an inherited risk factor for heart disease. Roughly one in five people has high levels, which are largely unaffected by diet or exercise.
U.S. guidelines now recommend testing Lp(a) at least once in adulthood
. Drugs targeting Lp(a) could give millions of people a new way to address that risk, but the clinical benefit of pharmacologic intervention still needs to be demonstrated.
Novartis’s recent pelacarsen trial
lowered Lp(a), but failed to meet its primary endpoint of reducing cardiovascular events. That said, we’re quite optimistic about other Lp(a) targeting drugs from Eli Lilly and Amgen now in trials.
Consumers don’t have to wait for the next drug. Knowing your Lp(a) can help you decide how aggressively to manage LDL and other heart risks. The question to ask: “Given my family history and risk, how low should I try to get my LDL – and would medication help me get there?” We see an opening for companies that connect testing to personalized treatment, help patients access medications, and track their progress. Knowing your numbers is the starting point. The opportunity is helping people act on them.
Wearables: Act 3
Wearables have become a daily habit for many consumers. In our survey, 54% of respondents use one, and 83% of those users check it every day. There’s still room to make that habit more useful: 55% of wearable users say their data has never prompted a behavior change or a doctor’s visit. What stood out to us is that nearly three in four would wear a second device if it measured something new. We expect the next wave of wearables to build on that curiosity, giving people more insight into their bodies and a clearer sense of what to do with it.
The first wave of wearables, led by Fitbit, got us counting our steps. The next generation – Oura, WHOOP, and Apple Watch – made checking our sleep, heart rate, and recovery routine.
Today’s third generation is being built to answer more specific questions about our health.
Clair
is developing a wearable designed to estimate hormone patterns without a blood draw.
Lumia
is building blood-flow tracking into earrings. These products give us new ways to understand parts of our health that have been difficult to track day to day.
The category leaders are expanding in this direction too: Oura now uses nighttime data to
flag patterns related to blood pressure
and lets users
log GLP-1 doses and side effects alongside their biometric data
, helping them follow how their body responds over time.
Deregulation is aiding this innovation. In January 2026, the FDA
updated its general wellness policy
, clarifying how certain non-invasive wearables can offer wellness insights without medical-device clearance. In June, it
closed its warning letter to WHOOP
after the company changed its Blood Pressure Insights feature and labeling. Oura is also offering
blood-pressure-related insights as wellness features
. There’s more room to build here, provided the products stay within the boundaries of wellness claims.
The bigger prize is a device small enough to wear without thinking about it, capable of reliably tracking a much wider range of biomarkers in real time. Imagine a non-invasive version of a CGM that measures far more than glucose. If we get there, we’ll have a fundamentally different understanding of what’s happening in our bodies in real time and mere step trackers will feel archaic.
Brain health & brain-computer interfaces
Neurotechnology could open up new ways to improve health and interact with consumer technology.
Elon Musk’s Neuralink has brought implantable brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) into public view. Its surgically implanted devices already enable clinical trial participants with paralysis to control computers by decoding intended movement from motor cortex activity. The company is also developing technology intended to restore some visual perception for people who are blind by stimulating the brain’s visual cortex.
Other emerging implantable approaches record from the brain’s surface without penetrating the underlying tissue, offering potential advantages for safety and long-term use. At UCSF, Dr. Edward Chang’s team uses high-density electrode arrays placed on the brain’s surface to record signals across several brain regions. In 2021, Chang’s team became the first to
decode full words directly from the brain activity of a person who was paralyzed and unable to speak
. More recently, the same team decoded
speech and gestures together
, showing the ability of a single implant to support both words and body language.
For other application areas, non-invasive approaches could bring neural interfaces to the masses even sooner. Companies are building brain-sensing patches, headbands, or hats with electrodes that can sense the state of the brain and are attempting to read it. Companies like
Conduit
,
Alljoined
, and
Aleph Neuro
are approaching neural decoding as a data scaling problem, collecting large datasets to train AI models that translate weak, noisy signals recorded non-invasively from the brain into text and images reflecting what someone is thinking or seeing. One day, we may be able to communicate ideas directly to AI agents without first putting them into words. But neurotechnology also has “write” applications relevant to health, where wearable devices could go further than just reading what’s happening in our brains and actually change brain activity itself. We’re seeing more and more neuro wearable devices coming to market that aim to improve sleep and focus through targeted stimulation of the brain or peripheral nerves.
This all sounds like science fiction, but we’re betting in our lifetimes, brain-computer interfaces and “wearables” for the brain will become a real consumer category and part of everyday life. Our survey suggests early consumer interest: 39% would use a non-invasive brain-sensing device for sleep, focus, or mood, and 29% for thought-controlled devices, with greater interest among men.
What Founders Should Take From This
The encouraging signal across these six areas is that consumers are ready to act. They’re seeking out tests, trying new products, and spending their own money on health.
That is the opportunity for founders: help people turn their interest in health into something useful in their lives. Start with a problem they care about, show that the product helps, and make it easy enough to keep using. Companies that do that well can grow with their customers as their needs change.
We believe some of the next great consumer health companies will be built around that relationship. If you’re building one, we’d love to hear from you.
