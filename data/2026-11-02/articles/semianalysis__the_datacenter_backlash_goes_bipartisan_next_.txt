The 2027 datacenter boom is already being built and next week’s midterms won’t stop it now. Roughly three-quarters of the 33.8 GW due across our top 20 states is already under construction, which is why we remain comfortable with the global +50 GW year we laid out in
our Oct. 16 article
. The campuses are taking shape. The election will change the rules around them, but much of the 2027 pipeline already has a substantial head start.
The fight is over 2028. Our model has 62.2 GW of US net additions that year, most of which hasn’t started construction. Most of it sits in states electing a governor on Nov. 3. This piece tracks the 13 states with the largest 2028 net additions in our model, setting aside Virginia and Mississippi, which don’t hold state elections this November. Together they account for 43.5 GW, or 70% of what we expect nationally, and 10 of the 13 elect a governor.

That leaves a 2028 pipeline still working through approvals, power projects and construction schedules as the rules change. In September, we argued that datacenter moratoriums
weren’t killing the US buildout
: of roughly 20 GW inside local restrictions, we estimated only 1.5 GW faced delay. Most exposed capacity already had protections or did not need the affected approval until after the pause expired. That broad conclusion has held up. What is changing is the reach of the restrictions given the growing anti-datacenter backlash.
Three things have worsened since. Texas, the largest US market, froze Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) permits on Sept. 21 on top of the interconnection-queue audit begun Aug. 3, bringing onsite power into the hold. Oregon expanded its earlier state-land freeze to air and water-discharge permits on Oct. 9, through July 1, 2027. And midterm candidates have campaigned on more restrictive positions: nine of the 20 nominees want a pause on new construction and the other 11 want conditions, and the split is about even by party. Both parties want developers to accept stricter conditions. The main difference is whether a developer can meet the new terms and keep building, or has to wait for the state to reopen approvals.
The biggest risk to our buildout forecast right now is a prolonged permitting freeze at the TCEQ. Texas alone adds close to 30% of US GW in 2028. A few months of lost time in that market can move the national number. We already flagged to
Datacenter Model subscribers
which companies are most at risk from the current pause. The question now is how long the pause lasts.
Gov. Greg Abbott (R), who is running for re-election in Texas, could reopen permitting once the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), the state’s grid operator, finishes its audit and the Texas Water Development Board completes its water-use review. ERCOT is targeting Dec. 10 to file its report, which does not by itself reopen permitting. A restart around then would leave developers dealing with a relatively short interruption, which some projects can absorb within their schedules. The audit still has to lead to a decision to resume issuance, and each permit still needs approval.
By contrast, Gina Hinojosa (D), his challenger, presents a more bearish approach: She would impose a moratorium until legislation is passed that gives communities a voice and protects household utility bills, as well as ends the tax incentives for datacenters. The regular legislative session in Texas takes place from Jan. 12 to May 31, 2027. By keeping approvals on hold throughout that session, the freeze would last about eight months from September. If you wait for the new rules to come into effect in late summer, it will be close to a year, that is five to nine additional months compared to restarting in December. A deal or a special session might happen sooner. However, a campus racing toward delivery in 2027 may not have that much time to spare.

This is the risk posed by the pro-moratorium candidates: a project cannot carry out work if it still requires an approval which the state has ceased to issue. If the candidate proposing a datacenter pause won in every state where one is running, those states would together contain roughly 30 GW of planned capacity scheduled for 2028. How much actually slips depends on the permits each project still needs and how long the pause lasts.

We are less concerned with the candidates who support conditions only. Most of their demands change the price and terms of development. A developer can fund the grid upgrade, change the cooling design or negotiate the community agreement and then move forward. That can be expensive and time-consuming, but it gives projects a route to approval. Several of the main conditions were already coming:
Submitting thorough water-usage studies: manageable for many projects, but the answer depends on cooling design and the local water supply.
Paying the infrastructure costs they create: We expect this to come anyway, with datacenter developers now
offering credits toward household electricity bills
More environmental transparency and dropping non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) –
Amazon Web Services (AWS) and Google have already started down this path
If the less restrictive candidates win, that exposure falls to 5.7 GW, mainly because Ohio and Oregon have proponents of a pause on both sides. That is a much smaller pipeline to test for delays. It would still be a tougher development environment: fewer tax breaks, more upfront commitments and less room to leave infrastructure costs with the utility’s other customers.

Which states are the most impacted depends on which companies are building. We interviewed six current and former employees of hyperscalers, including people who worked at Amazon, Microsoft and Google and are involved in datacenter development. Several said that hyperscalers can afford to pay the higher costs of new rules and can more easily bring or pay for their own power.
However, that doesn’t necessarily mean they will be able to deliver capacity on time. These projects still require their own permits and equipment. And having the money to pay for power transmission doesn’t make it available immediately. A moratorium can block any project that still needs approval, and it won’t be easy to shift development to another state: anywhere else would still require power, permits and construction schedules that can meet the same delivery timeline.
We made the same point from the other direction in
Stop Saying Half of 2026 US Datacenter Capacity Is Canceled
. Many projects being described as 2026 delays were speculative announcements that never belonged in a credible 2026 forecast. Here, the task is to identify which real campuses still need the approval a candidate wants to stop. A governor’s campaign platform is the start of that work. The permit, power phase and construction schedule tell us what happens to the megawatts.
The Growing Anti-Datacenter Sentiment Shaping US Politics
The datacenter backlash has moved faster than the industry expected. The AI boom has made these campuses visible, and candidates are tapping into concerns about jobs, noise, power rates and local resources. Those concerns now shape the terms on which developers can build.
The local candidates we interviewed all said that non-disclosure agreements that hide who developers are while they negotiate with local governments are a concern. “The public gets to know about all of this last,” said Rex Meyer (R), who is running for a seat on the Linn County Board of Supervisors in Iowa, which has datacenters and power projects being developed for Google and QTS within its borders. Meyer opposes the proposed Morgan Valley natural-gas plant at its Fairfax site, which would support the nearby Google and QTS datacenters.
Pam Marsh (D), who is up for re-election as a state representative in Oregon, said voter anxiety over AI is part of the problem. “We are starting to hear stories about people losing their jobs to AI. We’re looking at our kids, going: What can they train in? What should we be offering at our universities that will give people the best chance to make it in an AI world? And we don’t have clear answers to that yet.”
Marsh is open to a brief pause on datacenter construction in her state while tougher policies are written but doesn’t support the three-year moratorium recently proposed by her fellow Democrats, which she sees as effectively a ban.
In Georgia, Josh Tolbert (R), who is running for the Georgia Public Service Commission, the state’s utility regulator, believes datacenters should pay their own way. “If a datacenter causes a cost, that datacenter should pay it. Existing customers’ bills should not go up because of datacenters. It is that simple,” he said.
Development Risk at the State Level
State and local authorities control many of the decisions that determine where and how quickly datacenters get built, including land use, permitting, and access to power. Federal policy also plays a role via its transmission rules, environmental permitting, infrastructure costs and oversight.
Politicians at the federal level have occasionally pushed for measures that would require state action. In Michigan, both Senate candidates, Mike Rogers (R) and Abdul El-Sayed (D), have called for datacenter moratoriums, but neither would have the power to enact one from the Senate.
In our analysis, state elections, governors, legislatures and utility commissions have the most influence over policy. Although governors can at times delay certain approvals, more extensive restrictions or changes to taxes generally have to go through legislation, as we explained in our September
newsletter article on moratoriums
.
Governors
can act quickly by halting permits an agency hasn’t issued, changing review priorities and directing agencies to impose conditions within their legal authority. Abbott’s TCEQ freeze and the environmental-permit hold imposed by Tina Kotek (D) show how quickly that can affect a schedule. Existing permits protect the work they authorize, but a campus can still need approval for its next power phase. Appointments also matter: in Texas, Ohio and Pennsylvania, governors appoint utility commissioners, subject to Senate review; Ohio’s governor also shapes the Power Siting Board.
Legislatures
hold the money and the statute. The equipment sales-tax exemption is one of the biggest levers they wield, potentially worth billions of dollars per gigawatt, as it eliminates the sales tax on the servers, GPUs, networking gear, cooling systems, transformers and backup generators that fill a datacenter. Legislatures also write the statutory moratoria that outlast executive orders, which can be quickly repealed or reversed.
Utility commissions
decide what datacenters pay for power and the terms of service, including deposits, minimum bills, upgrade costs and exit fees. Commissioners are elected in Georgia, Arizona and Oklahoma and appointed in many other states. Staggered terms and confirmation requirements mean a governor’s victory doesn’t immediately change a commission’s majority. In states where the nation’s largest power transmission organization, PJM Interconnection, operates, governors don’t have much power over it.
Across the largest datacenter markets with state elections this November, we ask whether Nov. 3 can change the relevant decision-makers (governors, legislatures, and elected utility commissions), how much policy differs between the possible outcomes and how much capacity still depends on an affected decision. Local approvals remain a separate source of project risk. Our analysis focuses on state-level elections.

We divided states into three tiers based on their unstarted capacity exposure in 2028 and how directly the Nov. 3 midterm results could change the policies affecting that capacity. In this analysis, we distinguish existing restrictions from changes the election could bring.
Tier 1
includes states with large unstarted capacity exposure (>2 GW), where further restrictions are likely whoever wins. Texas, Ohio, and Arizona have restrictive candidates on both sides, while Georgia has a toss-up governor’s race and a Public Service Commission (PSC) majority on the ballot.
Tier 2
includes states with meaningful unstarted capacity (1.5-2 GW) where additional restrictions depend on a change in political control, through Pennsylvania’s Senate race or a Democratic win in Oklahoma’s governor’s race.
Tier 3
covers smaller unstarted capacity exposures (<1.5 GW), where more restrictive datacenter policies are highly likely: Illinois, Oregon and Iowa may face tighter policies whoever wins.
We’re leaving out Indiana, Missouri, Wyoming, and West Virginia from these tiers. Indiana and Missouri have large Republican majorities and supportive governors staying in office. Wyoming is likely to retain Republican control of both branches, while West Virginia’s governor and dominant Republican legislature remain aligned on datacenter development
